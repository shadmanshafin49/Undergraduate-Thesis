\begin{keybox}{The short version --- in plain language}
\small
\textbf{What this project is.} Three banks want to catch fraud. None of them is allowed to show its
customers' transactions to the other two. So instead of sharing data, they each train their own
detector and share only the \emph{trained numbers} (the model weights). A blockchain keeps the record
of who helped and who gets paid. The whole thing is called \textbf{FL-ADTCN}.

\smallskip
\textbf{Where we stood on 30 August.} The thesis had already been accepted. It had one genuinely new
idea, an honest list of things that did not work, and one big weakness underneath everything:
\textbf{every single claim had been tested on one dataset only} --- and that dataset does not tell you
which customer made which payment.

\smallskip
\textbf{What we did in these six days, and why:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Added a second dataset (BankSim), and kept the first one.} \emph{Why:} a claim that
  only holds on one file might be a fact about that file. Testing on two lets us tell the difference.
  \item \textbf{Ran a ``control'' experiment.} A control changes \emph{one} thing at a time so you can
  tell which thing actually caused an effect. Ours changed only the dataset and kept everything else
  fixed. \emph{Why:} our most exciting new finding had changed two things at once, so we could not
  know which one was responsible. \emph{Result: the control proved us wrong and we withdrew the claim.}
  \item \textbf{Audited the search algorithm the framework is named after (DB-BOA).} \emph{Why:} five
  completely different search algorithms had all returned exactly the same score, which is not
  something that happens by chance. \emph{Result:} the search had been marking its own homework --- it
  was testing itself on rows it had already trained on. We fixed it, re-ran it properly for 16.3 hours,
  and the original bad news about it survived.
  \item \textbf{Stopped quoting one of our own numbers} --- the ``privacy improvement factor''.
  \emph{Why:} we proved the number \emph{grows the harder you look for it}, so it describes our search
  effort, not the system. A number like that cannot safely be printed in a paper.
  \item \textbf{Put all of it into version control.} \emph{Why:} before 5 September roughly 20 hours of
  results existed only as untracked files on one machine.
  \item \textbf{11--12 September: took the other three datasets to measurement.} The rule that had kept
  us to two datasets (Rule~8) was suspended by the team lead, so the Fraud Detection Handbook, PaySim and
  IBM AMLSim were pre-registered and run. \emph{Why it needed care:} two of them arrived with a trap that
  would have decided the result on its own --- in PaySim, time order itself carries the label; in AMLSim,
  our own first attempt at generating the data tied the bank to the answer. Both were caught before any
  model saw them. \emph{Status, 12 September:} PaySim and AMLSim are fully measured, and all four of the Handbook's
  pre-registered items are now scored. \textbf{Eleven pre-registered predictions were wrong} --- two on
  PaySim, three in AMLSim's architecture grid, six on the Handbook --- one AMLSim criterion is reported as
  two scores with no verdict (as is the Handbook's stratified privacy half, though its customer half
  decides that item), and \textbf{the Handbook broke two defences that had held in every condition before it}
  (Section~\ref{sec:firstresults}).
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
\textbf{What it added up to.} \textbf{Three of our own headline claims died in six days, and all three
were killed by tests we ran on ourselves --- not by a reviewer.} That is the point of the period. The
work is smaller than it looked on 30 August, and far harder to argue with.
\end{keybox}

\section{How to read the numbers in this report}
\label{sec:scores}

This report quotes a handful of scores over and over. This section says what each one means, why we
chose it, and what a good or bad value looks like. If a number later in the document does not make
sense, the explanation is here.

\subsection{Why plain accuracy is almost useless to us}

Fraud is rare. In our first dataset only \textbf{0.17\%} of transactions are fraud. So a detector that
simply says ``not fraud'' to \emph{every single transaction} --- catching nothing at all --- scores
\textbf{99.83\% accuracy}. That number looks excellent and the detector is worthless.

This is not a hypothetical worry. It is exactly what bit us: in Section~\ref{sec:claims} a
privacy-protected model is destroyed so badly that it becomes useless (MCC falls from 0.428 to 0.012 on
PaySim), and plain accuracy reports the damage as a \emph{gain} of 1.16 percentage points.
\textbf{Accuracy hides failure on rare-event data.} That is why we lead with MCC instead.

\begin{warnbox}{Corrected 12 September: this paragraph had the sign wrong}
Earlier versions said plain accuracy recorded the ULB collapse as a \emph{fall} of 0.11\,pp. It was a
\emph{rise}: the collapsed model flagged nothing at all, so it scored the base rate, 99.83\%, against
FedAvg's 99.72\%. The regenerated ULB run of 12 September no longer shows the effect at all --- its
collapsed model flags 43{,}089 transactions and accuracy falls 75\,pp (Section~\ref{sec:firstresults}) ---
so the example now comes from PaySim, where it happens on the current files.
\end{warnbox}

\section{The system, in one page}

Before anything else it has to be clear what the ``thing'' is that we are judging --- because one of
the mistakes we made in this period was judging the entire thesis by one small piece of it.

\textbf{The thing is FL-ADTCN, a six-part system --- not a classifier.} Here is the whole idea in
everyday terms. Three banks (call them A, B and C) each want a better fraud detector. Each bank has its
own transactions and is not allowed to hand them to the other two. So instead of pooling the data, each
bank trains a detector on its own transactions and shares only the \emph{trained numbers} inside that
detector --- the model weights. Nobody's customer data ever leaves the building.

That much is standard federated learning. Around that weight-sharing step our system adds five more
mechanisms, and each one answers a different worry:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Differential privacy} --- \emph{``could someone reconstruct my customers from the
  numbers I shared?''} Deliberate random noise is added to the weights before they are shared.
  \item \textbf{Krum} --- \emph{``what if one of the banks is lying?''} A rule that inspects all the
  submitted weight sets and picks the single most typical one, so a sabotaged submission is never used.
  \item \textbf{Shapley values} --- \emph{``who actually helped, and how much should they be paid?''}
  A fairness calculation that splits the credit between the banks.
  \item \textbf{DB-BOA} --- \emph{``what settings should the detector use?''} An automated search over
  the detector's configuration, so the settings are not hand-guessed.
  \item \textbf{A Hyperledger Fabric blockchain} --- \emph{``who keeps the books, and can we trust
  them?''} A shared tamper-evident ledger records the payments, and picks which bank leads each round.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}{FedAvg --- the baseline everything else is measured against}
\textbf{FedAvg (Federated Averaging)} is the standard, original way to combine what the banks send back,
and it is the yardstick we compare against all through this report. It is exactly as simple as it
sounds: \textbf{take every bank's weights and average them, number by number.} If Bank A submits 0.4,
Bank B submits 0.6 and Bank C submits 0.5 for the same weight, the global model gets 0.5. Do that for
every weight in the model, send the averaged model back to all three banks, and repeat next round.
(In our code it is a plain, unweighted mean --- \texttt{byzantine\_robustness\_sweep.py:102}.)

\textbf{Its strength is that it is simple and it works.} \textbf{Its weakness is that an average has no
defence against a liar.} One bank submitting deliberately enormous numbers drags the average wherever
it likes, and the other two cannot outvote it, because averaging is not a vote --- everyone gets
included by construction. That single weakness is the reason Krum exists.

So whenever this report writes \textbf{``Krum $-$ FedAvg''}, it is asking one question:
\emph{what do we gain, or lose, by picking the most typical bank instead of averaging all of them?}
A positive number means Krum's protection also made the model better. A negative number means the
protection cost us accuracy. Both happen, and Section~\ref{sec:control} is about finding out why.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}{A mistake we made ourselves --- written down because it cost a day}
On 1 September we ran an internal review of our own direction, and it judged the \emph{entire} thesis
by a single thing: where our detector, ADTCN, ranked against six other detectors. \textbf{Every number
in that review was correct, and its conclusion was completely wrong.}

Here is why. Look at the table above: ADTCN's ranking backs \emph{none} of the six promises. It is the
foundation the other parts sit on, not a promise in itself. So a review built on that one ranking
silently threw away four of the six promises, and then recommended rewriting the paper around the two
that happened to be left.

The correction --- \textbf{judge the whole system, not one component} --- became a standing rule, and it
turned the recommendation completely round: the dataset work was \emph{under}-done, not over-done.
Everything in Section~\ref{sec:work} follows from that reversal.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Where we stood on 30 August --- what was good}

the reason the rest of it can be believed. Six things were already solid on 30 August.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{G\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{The central new idea is real, specific, and nobody else had described it.} Here it is in
  plain terms. The banks add noise to their shared weights to protect privacy. Separately, the system
  works out how much each bank contributed, and pays them accordingly. \textbf{What nobody had noticed
  is that the first of these breaks the second.} The privacy noise scrambles the contribution
  calculation so badly that the ranking of who helped most becomes meaningless --- so honest banks get
  paid the wrong amounts. Our fix is to move the noise: instead of noising the \emph{weights}, noise the
  \emph{published contribution score}. That protects privacy just as well and leaves the ranking intact,
  at a fraction of the privacy cost. Two mechanisms that are normally studied in separate papers turn
  out to interfere with each other, and that interference is the thesis's actual contribution.

  \item \textbf{The blockchain is really running, not simulated.} There is working chaincode on a real
  Hyperledger Fabric test network, and we measured it rather than estimating it: \textbf{2115.9\,ms}
  average time to commit one record (over 50 transactions), and a best throughput of \textbf{40.3
  transactions per second} at 10 concurrent clients. Push past that and it falls apart quickly --- half
  the transactions fail at 20 concurrent clients, 70\% at 40.

  \item \textbf{The attack defence was built where its maths actually applies.} Krum's guarantee only
  holds when there are enough honest participants relative to attackers, so we ran it in that regime
  and swept four different attack types. \textbf{The attacker was caught 8 times out of 8} --- in every
  condition until the Handbook, where a label-flipping attacker is selected at both federation sizes
  (Section~\ref{sec:firstresults}).

  \item \textbf{The work had already found and deleted its own invented numbers.} An earlier audit
  removed nine claims that no script had ever produced --- a 97.38\% accuracy, an MCC of 0.966, 85\,TPS,
  180\,ms, a comparison table of eight models, some statistical tests, and two more tables. They were
  written but never computed. That clean-out is what makes the numbers that survived worth reading.

  \item \textbf{Results that came out badly were kept as results.} The automated search does not beat a
  setting we picked by hand. The fast approximation of the fairness calculation is not reliable at the
  sizes we can check. The reward system does not stop a lone attacker or a free-rider. Krum has no
  guarantee with only three banks. \textbf{None of these was hidden, softened or spun.}

  \item \textbf{Every headline number could be traced back to a script and a data file.} There was no
  figure in the report that could not be regenerated from scratch.
\end{enumerate}

\section{How we decided to work}
\label{sec:method}

Before the experiments, the \emph{method} --- because changing how we worked was the single biggest
change of these six days, and it is what produced most of the findings. The experiments themselves were
fairly ordinary. The discipline around them was not.

\subsection{The standing rules}

A short list of rules governs every run. Here are the ones that actually did work in this period, in
plain language:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rule 1 --- Be novel and be completely honest. If those two ever pull in opposite
  directions, honesty wins.} Every other rule is a consequence of this one.
  \item \textbf{Rule 2 --- No number goes in a document unless a script produced it.} If the number does
  not exist yet, either run the experiment or delete the sentence. There is no third option.
  \item \textbf{Rule 3 --- Bad results stay bad.} When something does not work, that \emph{is} the
  finding. Do not go looking for a version of the experiment that makes it look better.
  \item \textbf{Rule 4 --- Do not shop for results, and write your prediction down first.} Never pick a
  dataset, a random seed or a split because it makes us look good --- and write down what you expect to
  happen \emph{before} you run, so you cannot quietly adjust the expectation afterwards.
  \item \textbf{Rule 8 --- A new dataset has to be able to prove something wrong, and covering all your
  claims beats piling up datasets.} One claim tested on three datasets while its four siblings sit on
  one is a lopsided table, and a reviewer will simply attack the four.
  \item \textbf{Rule 9 --- Judge the whole system, not one component.} (Added after the review mistake
  described on the previous page.)
  \item \textbf{Rule 11 --- Always say which score you are quoting, and why that one.} If a script
  computes two different numbers and the write-up quotes the nicer one without mentioning the choice,
  that is result-shopping wearing a disguise.
  \item \textbf{Rule 12 --- An experiment that could destroy a finding you already have is worth more
  than one that could add a new finding.} (Added 4 September, immediately after a three-hour control run
  destroyed our best claim.)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-registration, and what it bought}

``Pre-registration'' sounds formal but the idea is simple: \textbf{before starting a run, write down
what you expect to happen and what it would mean if you turned out to be wrong --- then never edit those
words.} When the results arrive you write them underneath, next to the prediction, whatever they say.

The reason to do it is that human memory is generous. Without it, a surprising result quietly becomes
``well, that is roughly what we thought'' by the time it is written up. With it, being wrong is a
recorded event you cannot talk your way out of.

Across seven runs in this period we have scored \textbf{44 predictions so far. Sixteen of them were
wrong} --- and those sixteen are the most valuable material in this report.

\subsection{First, close the three loose ends a reviewer would find (31 August)}

Before adding anything new, we closed the three problems from Section~\ref{sec:bad} that were cheapest
to fix and most embarrassing to leave open.

\textbf{First: the two contradictory scores (B3), and the answer was not what we had guessed.} Recall
the problem --- one of our files said the detector scored MCC 0.677, another said 0.313, and nobody knew
why. Thirteen full training runs settled it. Training is perfectly repeatable, \emph{provided} three
things stay fixed: the random seed, the version of the maths library, and \textbf{the number of CPU
threads}. That third one was the culprit. Two of our scripts were using different thread counts, so they
had never actually been doing the same arithmetic.

Why would thread count matter? Because adding up a million numbers in a different order gives a very
slightly different answer in floating-point arithmetic, and over 30 training epochs those tiny
differences compound. At the same seed, the tuned configuration scores \textbf{0.708, 0.746 and 0.800}
on 4, 2 and 8 threads respectively. So our old guess --- ``large batches make training unstable'' --- had
the right suspect and the wrong crime. \textbf{MCC 0.313 is withdrawn}: it does not reproduce under any
condition we could construct.

The honest replacement is not a single number but a range over five runs: \textbf{tuned 0.753
$\pm$ 0.055, hand-set default 0.706 $\pm$ 0.076, a difference of $+0.047$ with $p = 0.34$.} In plain
terms, \textbf{$p = 0.34$ means a gap this size would turn up about a third of the time by pure chance},
so we cannot claim the tuned version is better --- and, awkwardly for us, the \emph{default} is the more
stable of the two. The underlying cause was that the maths library was never pinned to a version; it now
is, and every detector number we report from here on has to state its library version and thread count
or it means nothing.

\textbf{Second: the real blockchain numbers went into the report (B4).} The invented 85\,TPS / 180\,ms
were replaced with the measured 2115.9\,ms average latency and the 40.3\,TPS curve. Measuring it also
exposed something the simulated version had been hiding: \textbf{our reward scheme pays a 15-token bonus
for committing a record in under 300\,ms, and the real network's floor is 2.1 seconds.} That bonus can
never be earned by anyone. It was a design mismatch that only appeared once real hardware was involved.

\textbf{Third: we deleted a duplicate definition.} The scoring formula existed in two versions --- an old
unbounded one still sitting in the code, and the bounded one described in the methodology chapter. There
is now exactly one, checked against the other over 2{,}000 random inputs and identical every time.

\subsection{The dataset question --- and the decision to keep ULB}
\label{sec:ulb}

This is the fork the whole rest of the work hangs on, so it is worth setting out carefully.

\textbf{The problem.} Our detector is built to spot suspicious \emph{patterns over time} in a person's
spending. To do that it reads 10 consecutive transactions at once. But ULB has no customer column, so
those 10 transactions belong to 10 unrelated strangers. \textbf{We had been testing a model designed to
learn habits on data that contained no habits} --- and it had duly lost to a much simpler model (0.770
against 0.459). We could not tell whether our architecture was genuinely bad or had simply never been
given a fair test.

\begin{decision}{1 --- Add a second dataset, and \emph{keep} the first one running too}
\qlabel{Question raised:} Do we \textbf{replace} ULB with a dataset that has customer identities, or
\textbf{keep} ULB and run both side by side?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} Keep ULB, add BankSim, and re-run \emph{every} comparison on both, through the
same code. BankSim was picked first out of the candidates because it is a single CSV file with 594{,}643
transactions, 7{,}200 frauds and 4{,}112 customers who each have about a hundred transactions --- small
enough to iterate on in minutes. (The full five-way comparison is Section~\ref{sec:fivedatasets}.)

\qlabel{Why:} Three reasons, and the third is the one that mattered.
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2pt,label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{ULB is what the accepted thesis stands on.} Drop it and every new result becomes
incomparable with the submitted report, leaving that report stranded.
\item \textbf{You can only show a result depends on the dataset if you measure both.} Swapping one
dataset for another does not broaden the evidence --- it just replaces one narrow story with a different
narrow story.
\item \textbf{Rule 4.} Keeping the dataset where our architecture looks good and dropping the one where
it looks bad is result-shopping, however reasonable the excuse sounds at the time. This is exactly the
situation the rule exists for, and it was tempting.
\end{enumerate}

\qlabel{Outcome --- and it genuinely cut both ways:}
\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{It paid immediately.} Entity-linked windows help \emph{every} architecture by
$+0.0999$ to $+0.1717$ MCC, so ``temporal complexity does not pay'' was a statement about
entity-\emph{less} windows, not about time. The differential-privacy collapse replicated on the second
dataset --- and its \emph{direction} flipped, which neither dataset shows alone.
\item \textbf{It also produced the two most uncomfortable findings of the period, and both are
ULB-only.} Keeping ULB is the only reason we discovered that the deployed hyperparameter search was
validating on memorised rows (Section~\ref{sec:obj13}), and the only reason we discovered that a ULB
result from 30 August no longer reproduces (Section~\ref{sec:obj17}). Had we dropped ULB, both defects
would still be in the work and neither would be known.
\item \textbf{The honest cost:} two ULB result files are currently not quotable, and one reproduction
failure is recorded as \emph{unexplained}. Keeping ULB bought us the knowledge and the bill together.
\end{itemize}
\end{decision}

\begin{warnbox}{A trap the second dataset nearly set --- caught before it ran}
BankSim's raw file lists fraudulent transactions \textbf{next to each other} within each day ---
3{,}635 adjacent fraud pairs in file order, against only 84 once you shuffle within the day. The reason
is mundane: the simulator that generated the file wrote its frauds out together. It says nothing
whatsoever about the real world.

But here is what it would have done to us. Our whole experiment compares customer-linked windows against
ordinary sequential ones. If we had built the sequential windows straight from file order, the
\emph{comparison arm} would have found that a fraud is followed by another fraud 50\% of the time ---
making it look strongly predictive, for a completely fake reason. \textbf{The comparison would have been
poisoned and the entity-linkage result destroyed, and nothing would have looked wrong.}

Shuffling within each day fixes it, and the corrected numbers behave exactly as they should: sequential
windows \textbf{0.0117} against a base rate of 0.0121 --- no signal at all, which correctly matches what
we see on ULB --- and customer-linked windows \textbf{0.3615}.

The general lesson, which is now a mandatory check before any new dataset: \textbf{if a dataset's time
column is coarser than its row order, the row order is carrying hidden information that came from how
the file was written, not from the world.}
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{The control that killed our best new claim (1--4 September)}
\label{sec:control}

The BankSim runs produced a genuinely exciting finding. Krum --- the rule that throws out a sabotaged
bank --- catches the attacker every single time, but it appeared to \textbf{charge us 1.5 to 12.6
percentage points of accuracy} for that protection whenever the banks held completely separate sets of
customers. We even had a tidy explanation for why. It was the most quotable sentence we had written all
week.

\textbf{It was also confounded}, and that word is worth defining because the rest of this section turns
on it. A comparison is \emph{confounded} when you change two things at the same time and then credit the
result to one of them. Every comparison we had run changed both the \textbf{dataset} (ULB
$\rightarrow$ BankSim) \emph{and} the \textbf{way customers were dealt out to the three banks}
(mixed together $\rightarrow$ kept separate). So when the number moved, we had no way of knowing which
of the two changes moved it --- and we had confidently credited the second one.

\begin{warnbox}{Correction, 11 September --- the ``partition effect'' was never the split alone}
The text above says the control changed \emph{only} the customer split. It did not, and we found out a
week later. None of the attack experiments tells the detector where one customer's history ends, so
each bank's model reads windows over its rows \emph{in whatever order the split leaves them}. The
stratified split shuffles the rows, so its windows are ten random transactions. The entity-disjoint
split keeps each bank's rows grouped by customer, so its windows are mostly one customer's own history.
Measured without training anything (\texttt{check\_partition\_windowing.py}): \textbf{0.00\% of the
stratified training windows come from a single customer, against 91.22\% under the entity-disjoint
split} (96.37\% on the Handbook, 88.76\% on AMLSim's native banks).

\textbf{What survives:} the withdrawal. Its decisive comparison is the \emph{dataset} column --- ULB
against BankSim, both stratified, both training on random windows --- and that comparison was clean.
\textbf{What changes:} every number in the \emph{partition} column above is the effect of entity-disjoint
banks \emph{plus} customer-linked training windows, not of the split alone. The test windows were the
same in both conditions, so only training moved. Read ``partition effect'' anywhere in this report as
``entity-disjoint split, which also changes what the detector trains on''.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{What survived, and it is worth having.} The security property is untouched and is now stronger
than before: \textbf{Krum catches the attacker 8 times out of 8 in all three conditions.} That is the
claim Krum is actually there to support, and it held on two datasets and three splits --- \textbf{until
12 September, when the Handbook produced the first exception} (Section~\ref{sec:firstresults}).

What we lost is the \emph{explanation} for the accuracy cost, not the cost itself. Krum can cost
accuracy; the amount tracks the dataset; and \textbf{we do not currently know why.} Writing that down is
uncomfortable and it is the correct thing to do --- a measured effect with no explanation is a result,
while an explanation invented afterwards to cover a measurement is not. Question~Q3 in Section~12
carries it as an open problem rather than papering over it.

\begin{warnbox}{A trap in the score itself, which we then had to map cell by cell}
While reading those results we noticed something impossible: in several runs, the model that had been
\textbf{attacked} scored \emph{higher} than the same model with no attack at all. An attack cannot
genuinely improve a model, so the score was misleading us.

Here is what was happening. Balanced accuracy treats catching fraud and catching non-fraud as equally
important. The attacker's noise happened to push the model toward flagging more transactions as fraud.
That caught more of the rare frauds, and balanced accuracy rewarded it --- even though the model had
become useless in every practical sense. \textbf{So the score was crediting the damage.}

We mapped exactly which results this affected, and the pattern is uncomfortable: \textbf{0 of 8 cells on
ULB, 2 of 8 on BankSim/stratified, and 5 of 8 on BankSim/entity-disjoint} --- worst, in other words,
precisely in the condition we had built our headline on. Every cell there worse than $-5$\,pp is a
contaminated one. Throwing out the contaminated cells, the real cost is $-4.73$ to $-1.48$\,pp, not the
$-12.57$ to $-1.48$ we had been quoting.

\textbf{And the important part: the withdrawal survives the clean-up.} On the three cells that are
uncontaminated in all three conditions, the same conclusion still holds --- so it is a real finding, not
an artefact of our own filtering.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Propagating a withdrawal is its own job (4 September)}

\textbf{A claim is not withdrawn when you decide it is wrong. It is withdrawn when the last script that
prints it has stopped printing it.} Those are different days, and the gap between them is where
retracted numbers come back to life.

So we searched the whole repository for the claim we had just killed, and found it \textbf{still alive
in two automatically generated drafts}, plus a separate over-claim sitting in the supervisor brief. We
fixed all of them \emph{in the scripts that write them}, not in the documents, and regenerated.

That search also turned up a bug worth naming, because it will happen again. \textbf{The offending
sentence was written by a script that reads only one result file.} Think about what that means: a script
looking at a single condition \emph{cannot possibly} tell whether an effect came from the dataset or
from the customer split --- it has never seen the other conditions. So any explanation it prints is one
its own evidence could never contradict. The rule we adopted: \textbf{a script may only assert something
its own input could have proved wrong.} Explanations now come only from the one script that reads all
three conditions together.

One accidental discovery along the way: \textbf{the supervisor brief's PDF had been silently stuck two
revisions behind its source for days.} The LaTeX installation was missing a font package, which was
quietly aborting every build --- so the file on disk kept being the old one. The document the supervisor
actually reads had not been rebuilt, and nothing anywhere reported an error. Fixed.

\subsection{The privacy budget: we measured it, and then retired it}
\label{sec:obj17}

This one needs a short setup, because the mistake in it is subtle and it is the most instructive thing
in the report.

\textbf{The setup.} The banks add noise to protect privacy. Too much noise and the ``who contributed
most'' ranking comes out wrong, so banks get paid the wrong amounts. The dial controlling how much noise
is added is \eps{} (epsilon): \textbf{small \eps{} means heavy noise and strong privacy; large \eps{}
means light noise and weak privacy.} Our contribution is that noising the \emph{published contribution
score} survives far heavier noise than noising the \emph{weights}. To put a number on ``far'', we
defined \epsst{}: \textbf{the heaviest noise setting at which the payments still came out mis-ranked.}
Compare the two channels' \epsst{} and you get an improvement factor --- we had been quoting ``60 times
better''.

\textbf{The problem with that number (B7).} We had only tested up to $\eps = 3000$, and the weight
channel was \emph{still failing} at the very top of that range. So the true crossover was somewhere
beyond where we had bothered to look. What we had was a floor --- ``at least 60 times'' --- and we had
printed it as a measurement. This was the only open item that could actually \emph{un-withdraw} a claim,
and it was the cheapest thing on the board to fix, so we fixed it.

\begin{decision}{4 --- Extend the grid \emph{and} the draw count, on one shared grid for all three conditions}
\qlabel{Question raised:} Extend the grid (test further out) or raise the repeat count (measure the
rate more precisely)? They fix different things.

\qlabel{Decision taken:} Both, and \textbf{every condition re-run on the same extended grid} --- 11
points out to $\eps=30000$, then a second pass at 1000 draws on the budgets that actually decide each
threshold. Full scope approved, about 4 hours of CPU.

\qlabel{Why:} Three columns on three different grids are not comparable. And a correction found
\emph{before} the run showed the two knobs are not interchangeable: more budgets test further out;
more draws tighten the estimate at a fixed budget.

\qlabel{Outcome:} The stated goal was met --- \textbf{all three conditions de-censored}, every factor
became a measurement rather than a lower bound. \textbf{And then the measurement turned out to be the
wrong thing to quote.}
\end{decision}

\begin{keybox}{The correction that reframed the whole objective --- and it came from reading our own code, not from a result}
Then, reading the code rather than the output, we realised something that invalidates the number
entirely.

\epsst{} is defined as \emph{the largest noise setting at which we caught a mis-ranking}. Look carefully
at what that definition does when you test harder:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Run more trials at each setting} and you can only ever \emph{find} more failures, never
fewer --- the extra trials are added to the old ones, they do not replace them.
\item \textbf{Test further out} and you can only ever add new candidates for the maximum, never remove
existing ones.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{So neither knob can ever push \epsst{} down. It can only go up, or stay put, forever.} Which
means our number was never converging on a true value at all --- it was simply recording \emph{how hard
we had looked so far}. Read it correctly, \epsst{} means ``the largest setting at which we happened to
catch a failure'', and it gets bigger every time anyone tries harder. That is a fact about our
measuring procedure, not about the system we were measuring.
\end{keybox}

And then the same day demonstrated it to us twice, in the most direct way possible. After the first
pass, BankSim/entity-disjoint looked like our best-supported column and we wrote down that it should be
preferred over ULB. \textbf{Six hours later, looking ten times harder found exactly one failure in a
thousand trials} at the top of its range. That single failure pushed its threshold up, pushed its factor
from $33\times$ to ``at least $100\times$'', and turned the number straight back into a floor. The
ranking of which condition to trust flipped back to where it had started --- \textbf{on the strength of
one event in a thousand.}

\begin{decision}{5 --- Retire the improvement factor as a headline}
\qlabel{Question raised:} We finally have measured numbers. Do we publish the factor?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} No. \textbf{Retire it}, and carry the contribution on two threshold-free

\qlabel{Why:} Rule~4. Picking whichever cut-off flatters the mechanism is result-shopping, and no
single number from this family is defensible. The mechanism does not need it.

\qlabel{Outcome --- what carries the claim now:}
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2pt,label=(\arabic*)]
\item \textbf{The ordering.} The output channel mis-ranks rewards no more often than the weight
channel --- 12 of 12 probed budgets, and 11/11, 11/11, 10/11 across the full grid. It is \emph{paired

And one genuinely reassuring result: the four fragile thresholds that had rested on a single inverted

\textbf{The fragility was about precision, never about existence.}

While verifying that the longer run contained the shorter one, we re-ran the nine historical budgets at

literal-by-literal; thread-count sensitivity was tested directly (all six weight tensors bit-identical).

stored result recorded the stack it ran under.}

\textbf{We recorded it as unexplained rather than guessing a cause.} The durable fix shipped the same

\emph{every} sweep result file. \emph{That diagnosis took a day and produced no number; the guard is
what stops the next one costing the same.}
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{The search the framework is named after (4--5 September)}
\label{sec:obj13}

The last and largest piece of work in the period. It started with the loose thread from B5 --- five
completely different search algorithms all reporting a score of exactly 5.0000 --- and it ended
somewhere considerably worse than we had expected.

\subsubsection*{First, what DB-BOA is supposed to do} The detector has settings that have to be chosen:
how many filters, how many training steps, and so on. Trying every combination on the real model would
take weeks, so DB-BOA does what every hyperparameter search does --- it trains a small, fast stand-in
model (a \textbf{surrogate}) for each candidate setting, scores it, and uses those scores to decide
where to look next. The whole method rests on one assumption: \textbf{that the score is a fair
reflection of the setting.}

\subsubsection*{The diagnosis} It was not. The scoring function \textbf{picked a fresh random sample of
rows and a fresh random seed every single time it was called}. So asking it to score the same setting
twice gave two different answers. Three measurements pinned down how bad this was:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The same setting, scored 25 times, ranged from 3.4497 to 5.0000} --- and hit the
  theoretical maximum once, purely by luck, with no searching involved at all. That alone explains the
  five identical 5.0000s.
  \item \textbf{Changing the random sample moved the score about as much as changing the model did}
  (1.0471 versus 1.0794). In other words, roughly half of what the search was ``learning'' was
  which candidates got lucky draws.
  \item \textbf{One of the two things it was searching over did nothing at all.} A formula meant to
  pick a batch size, \texttt{max(32, 1400 // spe)}, worked out to 32 for \emph{every single value} in
  the range being searched. So the advertised two-dimensional search was really one-dimensional, and
  every ``optimal'' value we had reported for that second setting was arbitrary.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Note which way this cuts.} It would be easy to read ``the search was broken'' as an excuse ---
\emph{our method never really got a fair trial}. It is the opposite. A search that cannot reliably rank
its own candidates is effectively returning one at random, and \textbf{a randomly chosen setting tying
with a sensibly hand-picked one is exactly what you would expect.} The broken search does not rescue the
negative result; it explains it.

\subsubsection*{Then the shipped path was checked, and it was worse}

\begin{warnbox}{We had audited a script --- but not the code that actually runs}
Our diagnostic script built its stand-in model from a pool of 6{,}000 rows. \textbf{The real pipeline
uses a different function, and that one returns 3{,}000.} We had spent three days auditing something
adjacent to the thing that ships.

Follow the arithmetic on the real one. ULB is 0.173\% fraud, so a 3{,}000-row pool contains about
\textbf{5 fraud transactions}. The stand-in model demands at least 30 to train on. It cannot conjure 25
more, so it drew 30 ``with replacement'' --- meaning it took the same 5 transactions over and over,
about six times each. Then it split those 30 rows into 21 for training and 9 for testing. Since all 30
were copies of the same 5, \textbf{all 9 test rows were duplicates of rows it had just trained on.}

The model was being tested on its own training data. It scored perfectly. \textbf{The 5.0000 was not
luck --- it was memorisation}, and it would have happened every time.

Two things make this worse. First, \textbf{the repair we had already designed would not have fixed it}:
our plan was to force the split to include fraud on both sides, which with only 5 unique transactions is
precisely the mechanism that puts copies on both sides. Second, BankSim was completely clean throughout
(38 and 75 unique frauds), so \textbf{a project using only the second dataset would never have seen
this.} The rule we took away: \textbf{audit the code path that ships, not one that resembles it.}
\end{warnbox}

\begin{decision}{6 --- Fix the pool by enlarging it, not by re-weighting or clamping}
\qlabel{Question raised:} Three ways to stop the surrogate validating on memorised rows. (a)~Enlarge the
pool so 30 genuinely distinct fraud rows exist at the real rate. (b)~Oversample fraud \emph{into} the
pool. (c)~Never sample with replacement --- accept whatever few unique frauds exist.

\qlabel{Decision taken:} (a). Pool raised from 3{,}000 to 36{,}000 rows for both datasets, plus a
runtime warning that fires if the replacement branch is ever taken again.

\qlabel{Why:} (b) would make the surrogate see a fraud rate that is not the dataset's --- the exact
defect the function was written to avoid. (c) would leave ULB's surrogate training on \textbf{five}
fraud rows, which is honest and useless. Only (a) keeps the rate real \emph{and} the positives distinct.
The warning was added because \textbf{the cost of this defect was its silence}: a config value alone
would fix today's datasets and let the next one reintroduce it without a sound.

\qlabel{Outcome:} Verified 0 of 11 validation positives leaked, on both datasets, in both modes.
\end{decision}

\subsubsection*{The repair, and three defects it would have shipped with}

Fixing the scoring function turned out to be the easy part. Applying that fix turned up \textbf{three
separate ways the repair would have quietly destroyed the evidence for its own necessity} --- all three
found by checking the output rather than trusting the exit code:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The patch \textbf{silently re-pointed the diagnostic script} --- the one that produced this
  entire objective's evidence base --- at the new default mode. It would have reported a within-seed
  standard deviation of \textbf{exactly 0.0000} and read as \emph{``the objective was always fine''},
  which is the precise opposite of the finding. It now pins the old behaviour explicitly, with the
  reason written at every call site so it is not ``simplified'' away later.
  \item The same silent switch in the base-paper comparison script, whose stored results \emph{are} the
  5.0000 evidence. It now stamps the mode into its own output and \textbf{refuses to overwrite} a file
  recorded under a different one. A warning would not do --- a warning scrolls off the top of a six-hour run.
  \item A pre-existing filename collision in which two separate result tracks overwrote each other,
  leaving only whichever ran last. \textbf{This was the third instance of the same class of bug} in a
  fortnight: an artefact whose only copy lives somewhere the next run can destroy.
\end{enumerate}

We also verified the old behaviour is reproduced \textbf{bit-for-bit} --- three configurations agreeing
to ten decimal places against a saved copy of the pre-patch module --- so the comparison runs against a
live baseline rather than a remembered one.

\begin{decision}{7 --- Run the comparison at full deployment budget, against the recommendation}
\qlabel{Question raised:} The side-by-side could run cheaply (about 1.1\,h, matched to the base-paper
budget) or at the \emph{deployed} search budget (about 16.7\,h). The written recommendation was the
cheap option.

\qlabel{Decision taken:} \textbf{The expensive one} --- population $20\times30$, three surrogate
protocols $\times$ three seeds, BankSim only, run detached overnight.

\qlabel{Why:} Only the deployed budget yields configurations comparable to the one actually shipped in
our results (142 filters / 76 steps-per-epoch). A cheaper run would have answered a question we were not
asking. BankSim only, because at that moment ULB's pool did not describe its own deployment, so a ULB arm
would have cost 17 hours to produce a number needing a caveat longer than the result.

\qlabel{Outcome:} 16.3 hours, 9 of 9 searches completed, clean exit. \textbf{Not one of the nine
searched configurations beats a hand-set 128 filters / 150 steps-per-epoch.} If this run ever has to be
repeated, repeat it at the same budget --- do not ``optimise'' it back down.
\end{decision}

\subsubsection*{Two findings that inverted what we had predicted}

\begin{keybox}{1. What fixes a noisy search is not freezing the randomness --- it is sharing it}
This one surprised us completely, and it is the most useful thing in the report for anyone doing similar
work.

There are two obvious ways to stop random scoring from wrecking a search. \textbf{(a) Freeze the
randomness:} use the same fixed seed every time, so the scoring function stops being random.
\textbf{(b) Share the randomness:} let it be random, but score \emph{every candidate on the same random
sample}, so they are all judged on the same test. We expected (a) to be the fix, and we had predicted
that neither would fully work.

Look at how widely each mode's chosen answer scattered across three runs (the setting ranges from 5 to
255): \texttt{legacy} (broken) scattered by \textbf{66}. Merely freezing the seed --- \texttt{deterministic}
--- scattered by \textbf{81}, which is \emph{worse than doing nothing}. Sharing the sample ---
\texttt{averaged} --- scattered by \textbf{12}.

\textbf{Freezing the randomness achieved nothing. Sharing it collapsed the scatter almost to zero.} The
reason is simple in hindsight: a frozen seed still gives every candidate a \emph{different} sample, so
the comparison between them is still corrupted. What matters is not that the noise is repeatable, but
that all candidates face the identical test. That detail was added to our code almost as an
implementation convenience, and it turns out to be the half of the repair that does all the work.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}{2. The mode that works best at searching is the one that generalises \emph{worst}}
Now the sting. Having fixed the search so it finally converges, we scored all three modes on fresh data
they had never seen. The winner should have been the fixed one. It came last:
\texttt{deterministic} 3.5857, \texttt{legacy} (broken) 3.5552, \texttt{averaged} (fixed)
\textbf{3.4237}.

The explanation is straightforward once you see it. Sharing the same three samples across all candidates
is what let the search rank them reliably --- but it also means the search can now relentlessly optimise
\emph{for those three particular samples}. And it did: it converged on high-filter settings (137, 146,
149) that fit those samples beautifully and generalise worse. \textbf{Making the scoring function
rankable made the search much better at exploiting it.}

The random noise in the broken version had been acting as an accidental brake on overfitting. That is a
real and slightly funny finding, and it is emphatically \textbf{not} an argument for keeping the broken
version --- a brake that works by accident is not a brake you can rely on.
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection*{And one defect the repair itself revealed}

Our repair only touched the stand-in model. The real deployed model still uses the old formula --- and
here is the twist: on the real model, which trains on roughly 400{,}000 rows rather than 2{,}000, that
formula does \emph{not} collapse to a constant. \textbf{The second setting was dead in the search but
alive in the deployed model.}

That is worse than simply wasting one of the two search dimensions. It means the search was blind to a
setting the real model genuinely responds to --- it could not have found a good value for it even in
principle. Whether to change the deployed formula is \textbf{deliberately still an open question},
because changing it would invalidate every model we have trained so far, and that is not a decision to
take quietly at the end of a section.

\textbf{The negative result got stronger by being attacked.}

\subsection{All five datasets, taken to measurement (11 September)}
\label{sec:allfive}

On 11 September the team lead suspended Rule~8. Every dataset assessed in
Section~\ref{sec:fivedatasets} is now to be run and compared, including the two that rule had set aside.
That changes the scope, not the standard: Rules~2, 4, 6 and 11 still apply in full, so each new dataset
got a reconnaissance pass, a loader with its own acceptance checks, and a written, approved
pre-registration \emph{before} any model trained on it. This section records first what the preparation
found --- in two of the three cases, a trap that would have decided the result on its own --- and then,
in Section~\ref{sec:firstresults}, what the PaySim and AMLSim runs measured.

\begin{decision}{12 --- Run every assessed dataset}
\qlabel{Question raised:} Rule~8 had limited us to datasets that could overturn a claim, which is why
PaySim was rejected and AMLSim deferred. Keep that gate with eight days left?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} The team lead suspended Rule~8: all five datasets are run and compared.

\qlabel{What it does not change:} a dataset that lacks the structure to test a claim is still reported
as not testing it (Rule~6) --- never counted as a pass. PaySim has no banks, so it tests no
entity-disjoint split; AMLSim has three banks, so the two sweeps that need five to twelve cannot run on
its native split. Both limits are written into their pre-registrations.
\end{decision}

\begin{decision}{13 --- Where to run three more datasets}
\qlabel{Question raised:} The laptop is booked for two days (the ULB re-run, then the Handbook). PaySim
and AMLSim cannot finish on it before 16 September. Run them elsewhere, or subsample them?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} ULB and the Handbook stay on the laptop --- the environment BankSim ran in, so
those three stay comparable. PaySim and AMLSim run on Kaggle's free CPU sessions, five at a time.

\qlabel{Why:} Kaggle is no faster per job (a probe measured a 4-vCPU Xeon at 2.2\,GHz); its value is
running five jobs at once. The price is a second environment, and this project has already lost a day
to one it could not reconstruct. So every Kaggle job installs the laptop's exact library versions (the
probe showed they install cleanly), pins four threads, and records the versions it actually imported and
a hash of the exact code it ran. Each dataset runs entirely on one machine, so its own comparisons never
mix environments; a cross-dataset column involving PaySim or AMLSim is also an environment comparison,
and is labelled so. Python itself cannot be matched (3.12 against 3.13), and that is recorded rather
than hidden.

\qlabel{Outcome so far:} the first end-to-end test job failed usefully, twice over: Kaggle silently drops
empty folders, so the job could not write its results; and the thread setting did not take (the job
reported 2 threads, not 4). Both were fixed before any real job was sent. Two more faults surfaced
overnight, and both are fixed. A transient error from Kaggle's command-line tool was misread as a failed
job; in the confusion one job was sent into a sixth, forbidden slot. Kaggle refused it, but the tool
reported success, so the job sat unrun for four hours until a status check found it missing. The job tool
now counts a job as sent only on Kaggle's explicit success message.
\end{decision}

\begin{decision}{14 --- IBM AMLSim: the real simulator, not a substitute}
\qlabel{Question raised:} AMLSim is a Java simulator that needs a build and a configuration before a
single transaction exists. IBM also publishes a newer, pre-generated dataset (AMLworld) with bank
columns. Use that instead?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} The team lead chose the real simulator, with its shipped 10K parameter set and
three banks at our own 50/30/20 shares.

\qlabel{Why it needed care:} choosing a simulator's configuration is a researcher degree of freedom --- a
dataset can be tuned towards a result as surely as a model can. So the one change is applied by a
script (\texttt{data/make\_amlsim\_config.py}) that refuses to run on anything but the pinned upstream
commit, and the whole pipeline is one script (\texttt{data/generate\_amlsim.py}) that records a SHA-256
for every file it writes. Two repairs were forced rather than chosen, and both were shown not to touch
content: the shipped configuration lacked three keys the pinned code reads (copied from the repository's
own root configuration, nothing overwritten), and upstream Python writes line endings on Windows that
crash the Java half (the pipeline strips them and asserts that only those bytes went and the record
count is unchanged). MASON, the simulation library, is on no public package registry and its release
carries no jar, so it was built from its tagged source.
\end{decision}

\begin{warnbox}{Our own first AMLSim dataset tied the bank to the answer --- caught before any model saw it}
The first derivation gave each bank a contiguous block of accounts. The reconnaissance pass then showed
\textbf{94.6\% / 5.1\% / 0.2\%} of transactions sent from the three banks, and \textbf{43\%} of the third
bank's transactions being laundering. The simulator fixes an account's activity by its position in the
account list, so contiguous blocks had tied bank to activity --- and, through that, to the label. A
detector could have ``found'' laundering just by learning which bank a transaction came from.

Interleaving the banks every ten accounts fixed it: \textbf{50.1 / 30.0 / 19.9\%} of transactions, and the
bank columns now predict nothing (MCC $-0.001$). The first dataset was discarded; nothing was ever
trained on it. What remains is a genuine, smaller difference, reported rather than engineered away: the
laundering rate is 0.24 / 0.37 / 0.57\% across the three banks.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{decision}{15 --- PaySim: which rows, which features}
\qlabel{Question raised:} All 8{,}213 PaySim frauds sit in two of its five transaction types. Keep all
6.36\,M rows, or only those two types? And the balance columns --- use them?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} The two types (2.77\,M rows, every fraud) --- a call the team lead delegated. The
four balance columns as given (the team lead's call), but none of the derived ``balance error'' terms
that are PaySim's known leak, and not the simulator's own fraud flag, which is a subset of the label.

\qlabel{Why:} the other three types contain \textbf{zero} fraud in 3.59\,M rows. Keeping them only adds
negatives the type column alone classifies --- exactly the accuracy inflation this report warns about in
Section~\ref{sec:scores}. It is the standard PaySim scope, and it keeps every job inside Kaggle's 12-hour
limit. \textbf{A pre-registered sensitivity run} repeats the federated comparison on all 6.36\,M rows with
the same split, so ``did the subset change your conclusion?'' will be answered by a measurement, not an
argument.
\end{decision}

\begin{warnbox}{PaySim's time order carries the label}
Every earlier dataset had the same shape: shuffle the rows inside each time step, and the previous
transaction tells you nothing about the next one (ULB, BankSim, the Handbook: a lift of about
1$\times$). PaySim breaks that. Even after the shuffle, a fraud is followed by another fraud
\textbf{151.6$\times$} more often than chance. The reason is in the simulator: after hour 400 the ordinary
traffic collapses while the fraud continues, so fraud climbs from 0.14--0.26\% of transactions to 0.86,
1.24 and then \textbf{3.71\%} --- and most of the test period sits in that tail.

So on PaySim the ``global window'' arm is \emph{not} a no-signal control, and linking by receiving account
carries almost nothing (1.30$\times$; senders cannot be linked at all --- 99.87\% appear once). The
pre-registration therefore predicts, in advance, that global windows will beat linked ones here --- and
says that this would be read as the artefact, not as evidence that linkage fails.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{Two PaySim predictions were wrong --- and both point towards ULB, not BankSim}
\textbf{Krum's utility cost is not a general property of non-ULB data.} We predicted PaySim would repeat
BankSim, where choosing one bank's update instead of averaging costs accuracy. On every PaySim cell whose
baseline is clean, Krum \emph{protected} accuracy instead: the scaled and Gaussian attacks cut unprotected
FedAvg by 7.6 to 19.2\,pp, and Krum lost none of it. That is ULB's pattern. Neither named falsifier
threshold was reached either (Krum ahead in 4 of 8 cells, where 5 were needed), so this is a failed
prediction leaning towards the alternative, not a clean reversal. Whether the cost is specific to BankSim
now waits on the Handbook.

\textbf{Linking a receiver's transactions helps even where time order carries the label.} We expected
PaySim's 151.6\x{} time clustering to make plain time-ordered windows win. Instead, linking by receiving
account won for six of seven architectures. Our own ADTCN gained the most from the link ($+0.119$ MCC) and
still ranked only fourth; under plain time order it ranked last.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{keybox}{AMLSim: the rows carry nothing, the links carry the signal}
Under a shuffled split the federation catches none of the 106 laundering cases in the test set (MCC
$-0.011$), exactly as pre-registered: AMLSim's rows carry no signal. Under the three native banks, where
each bank's training windows are its own senders' histories, FedAvg reaches MCC $0.158$. That lift moves
the split \emph{and} the windows together, so it is never quoted as a partition effect.

With the federation off the floor, both incentive results replicate on a fourth condition. Isolation fires
on 2 of 3 lone attackers and helps on none, and two colluding banks are still caught ($+6.4$ and
$+28.6$\,pp). For the privacy channel, the two readings of the pre-registered criterion disagree: the
output channel inverts no more often than the weight channel at 11 of 11 noise levels, and strictly less
often at 8 of 11. The other three are levels where both channels rank the banks perfectly. By the team
lead's rule, \textbf{we give both scores and no verdict} (Decision~19).

\emph{Not pre-registered:} the fair-share split under the native banks is $[0.550, 0.316, 0.135]$,
tracking the banks' sizes --- the first condition in this project where the incentive layer has something
non-uniform to attribute.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}{AMLSim's architecture grid: the links carry everything --- and three of five predictions were wrong}
As pre-registered, linking a sender's or a receiver's transactions lifts every one of the seven
architectures from the floor (MCC about 0.003) to between 0.12 and 0.36. Three details went the other way:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The sender link helped more than the receiver link, for all seven models.} The
  reconnaissance figures (receiver 41.5\x, sender 26.0\x) had predicted the reverse. The grid's own
  measure, taken on the orderings the models actually saw, ranks the two links the other way round
  (187\x{} against 178\x).
  \item \textbf{Our ADTCN came first under plain time order}, by 0.0002 MCC with every model at the
  floor --- a ranking of noise. The prediction said ``under any ordering'', so it is scored as wrong.
  \item \textbf{Its attention layer beat the no-attention version under both linked orderings}, which we
  had predicted it would not --- by less than one seed's spread each time.
\end{itemize}
The named alternative did not happen: ADTCN was not first under a linked ordering. It came second and
fifth; ResNet-1D led both.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The Handbook's first sweep breaks a claim we had called safe}
The Handbook suite started at 04:03 and its first sweep landed at 06:11; the rest runs for about another
29 hours. Two results are already in.

\textbf{(a) is wrong, in the same direction as PaySim.} Krum costs balanced accuracy in 4 of 8 cells
against the 5 predicted, and in 1 of the 5 clean cells against the majority predicted. Neither named
falsifier threshold is reached either. Taken with PaySim, the utility cost now looks like a \emph{BankSim}
property: negative in 7 of 8 and 8 of 8 there, against 1 of 8 on ULB, 2 of 8 on PaySim and 4 of 8 here.

\textbf{And Krum did not reject the attacker 8 times out of 8.} Every condition until now rejected all
eight, and the pre-registration called that ``held by construction, not a finding''. On the Handbook it is
6 of 8: under \emph{label-flip}, at both federation sizes, the poisoned organisation is the one Krum
picks. It is not an outlier in weight space --- it scores \emph{below every honest organisation} (894
against 914--995 at five banks; at seven banks both attackers rank first and second). The three attacks
that change an update's magnitude are still rejected, their scores two to three orders of magnitude out.
The cost is real: Krum's accuracy falls 18.64 and 11.77\,pp below its own no-attack reference.

Why a label-flipped model sits closer to the centre than the honest ones is \textbf{not established
here}, and we assert no mechanism. What changes today is the claim: the security property holds in five
of six conditions, not everywhere.

\textbf{Confirmed on the second partition (12:23).} Under the Handbook's customer split the attacker is
caught 7 of 8 --- the same attack, label-flip, at seven banks, with the poisoned model again scoring below
every honest one, and Krum's accuracy falling 13.4\,pp below its own reference. At five banks that attack
did not capture Krum. Across the Handbook's two partitions the defence misses \textbf{2 of 16 cells}, so
this is not an artefact of one split.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The same blindness breaks the incentive layer's collusion catch}
The Handbook's second sweep, at 07:18, makes the point twice over. \textbf{No organisation is ever
isolated --- in any of the six scenarios} --- so every accuracy gap is exactly zero, including the
two-attacker collusion cases that were caught in all five earlier conditions (by $+6.4$ to $+89.7$\,pp).

The mechanism is visible in a single number. The layer lowers an organisation's reputation when its share
of the fair-contribution split falls below an equal share (a third, with three banks). On BankSim the
attacked bank scores 0.18, so its reputation hits the floor by round 3 and it is dropped with no tokens.
On the Handbook the attacked bank scores \textbf{0.336 against an equal share of 0.333} --- so its
reputation drifts \emph{upward} over the twelve rounds, while an honest bank, at 0.326, drifts down. The
defence is not merely silent here; it is very slightly pointed the wrong way.

So on this dataset \textbf{both defences fail on the same attack for the same reason}: Krum's distance
test and the incentive layer's contribution test each rate a label-flipped model as normal or better. The
pre-registered lone-attacker prediction (``improves accuracy in 0 of 3 cases'') does still hold here, but
it holds \emph{vacuously} --- nothing fires, so nothing can help. That is worth considerably less than the
five conditions where the mechanism fired and still did not help, and it is quoted that way.

\textbf{The second partition made it worse, not better (13:17).} Under the customer split nothing is
isolated either --- twelve scenarios across the two partitions, every gap exactly zero --- but here the
contribution split is no longer flat, and it points the wrong way. In all three lone-attacker runs the
layer ends up ranking an \emph{honest} bank as the worst contributor: against always-fraud the attacker
finishes at reputation 1.007 while an honest bank falls to 0.529, and against label-flip the attacker
finishes at 1.044 while an honest bank sits at \textbf{0.507 --- seven thousandths above the threshold at
which the system expels a participant.} A slightly longer run would have thrown out the honest bank and
kept the attacker. Against two colluding banks the ordering is at least correct, but far too slow to reach
the threshold within the twelve rounds. Why the attribution mis-ranks here is \textbf{not established},
and we assert no cause.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The Handbook's own models sit just below the floor --- what that does and does not change}
The federated ablation finished at 10:27 and puts the Handbook's plain federated model at \textbf{MCC
0.0439}, six thousandths under the 0.05 floor line. Every table and figure in this report now marks that
column, exactly as it marks AMLSim's shuffled split.

\textbf{It is not AMLSim's kind of floor.} AMLSim's model catches 0 of 106 laundering cases --- inert. The
Handbook's catches \textbf{1{,}422 of 3{,}177} (44.8\% recall) at 1.62\% precision: weak, and swamped by
86{,}547 false positives, but not nothing.

\textbf{What we are not doing is re-labelling this morning's results.} The Handbook's pre-registration has
no floor clause --- that was AMLSim's item (f), written for AMLSim before AMLSim ran. Converting three
already-scored items into ``untestable'' after seeing the ablation land just under a threshold borrowed
from another dataset would turn a failed prediction into a non-result. The items stay scored as written,
and the floor is reported beside them (Decision~21).

\textbf{What it does change is how much they carry.} A weak model is a plausible reason why neither
defence could separate the attacker here, and both failures are consistent with it.

\textbf{The customer split, which ran next, did not settle it.} We expected that split to be far stronger,
because entity grouping lifts every other dataset in this project. In the \emph{sweeps} it is not: their
no-attack federated model scores 63.4 and 62.1\% balanced accuracy against 63.4 and 60.8\% on the
stratified split --- the same within noise. So the hypothesis stays untested, and what the second
partition did establish is that the security failure is not confined to one split.

\textbf{Correction, added at 16:01 when the customer ablation landed.} An earlier draft of this box drew a
second conclusion --- that on the Handbook, grouping by entity does not lift the models at all. \textbf{On
the ablation that is wrong, and we are striking it.} With every other setting held identical and only the
partition changed, the plain federated model goes from \textbf{MCC 0.0439 to 0.1500} --- more than three
times better, and over the floor. What does not lift is the \emph{window} grouping, which is a different
knob: the grid loses MCC under both linked orderings. \textbf{These two point in opposite directions on
this dataset}, which is precisely the separation our own control forced us to make in September. Note the
comparison is not clean --- the sweeps score balanced accuracy on unlinked windows, the ablation scores
MCC on customer-linked ones --- so the lift is established \emph{for the ablation}, and why the two
disagree we do not know.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{keybox}{Weight-channel privacy destroys the model in every condition that can show it}
Nine conditions now have a federated ablation. In the seven where the plain federated model works at all
(MCC \atleast{} 0.05), adding privacy noise to the weights drops it below that floor in \textbf{all seven}:
ULB, both BankSim splits, both PaySim row scopes, AMLSim's native banks and --- new at 16:01 --- the
Handbook's customer split. The other two, AMLSim's shuffled split and the Handbook's stratified split,
start at the floor and cannot show anything. How the collapse looks on accuracy is not stable, and
\textbf{it has now twice been invisible}: on PaySim accuracy \emph{rose} 1.16\,pp while MCC fell from 0.428
to 0.012, and on the Handbook's customer split it \emph{rose} 0.64\,pp while MCC fell from 0.150 to
$-0.005$. Changing nothing but which PaySim rows are included swings the collapsed model's accuracy from
98.70\% to 28.22\%, with MCC near zero both times.
\end{keybox}

\begin{warnbox}{Two more things the Handbook's customer ablation says, one of them about our own detector}
\textbf{Krum costs accuracy here after all.} On the stratified split Krum was one of only two conditions
where it \emph{helped} the ablation ($+0.0081$). On the customer split it costs $-0.0411$ MCC. So the list
of conditions where Krum helps shrinks back to ULB and the Handbook's own floor-bound stratified split,
and it is negative in 7 of the 9 conditions we have measured.

\textbf{And our own detector degenerates on this split.} DB-BOA-ADTCN reaches MCC $+0.0022$ by flagging
\emph{everything}: all 3{,}177 frauds caught, 351{,}280 false positives, specificity 0.05\%, overall
accuracy \textbf{0.95\%}. That is not a weak detector, it is a constant-positive one, and we would rather
say so in a box than leave it to be found in a table.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The privacy channel ordering fails for the first time --- on the Handbook's customer split}
The claim this project is built around is that a bank should publish a \emph{noised contribution} rather
than noised model weights, because the published channel scrambles the reward ranking less. We
pre-registered it as: the published channel inverts rewards less often at \textbf{at least 10 of the 11
noise levels, on each partition}. The Handbook's customer sweep finished at 14:09 and reaches \textbf{9 of
11}.

\textbf{This one is not a counting argument.} The stratified half had been ambiguous --- 10 of 11 if ties
count in the claim's favour, 9 of 11 if they do not --- so we reported it as two scores and no verdict.
The customer half has \textbf{no ties at all}, so both ways of counting give the same 9. And because the
prediction was written ``on each partition'', \textbf{the item fails whichever way the stratified half is
read}. We are not using the customer result to settle the stratified one: that half still gets quoted as
two scores.

\textbf{What did not fail is the direction.} At the noise level the pre-registration singles out, the
published channel still ranks banks far better than the weight channel does, on both splits ($+0.057$
against $+0.835$ stratified, $+0.096$ against $+0.630$ customer). The criterion that fails is the
inversion \emph{rate}, and it fails at the two gentlest noise settings, where \emph{both} channels are
already inverting 75--88\% of draws --- points our pre-registration said in advance would count against
us, and named as such.

\textbf{Two further cautions from the same file.} On the customer split the weight channel is
\emph{censored}: 29 of 100 draws still invert at the strongest noise we tested, so its threshold lies
outside the grid entirely. And the published channel's threshold there is decided by \textbf{2 draws out
of 100}, against the fragility line of 5 that this project set for itself --- so that number is not
quotable, and we do not quote it.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The window grid: the entity that concentrates fraud the most produces the \emph{worst} windows}
The Handbook's 21-cell grid --- 7 architectures $\times$ 3 window orderings, 3 seeds each --- finished on
Kaggle and was merged at 15:09. \emph{Provenance matters here:} these cells ran on Kaggle, not the laptop,
so every comparison inside the grid is sound, but Handbook-versus-BankSim \emph{architecture} comparisons
are now environment comparisons too, and are marked wherever they appear.

We predicted that entity-linked windows would beat unlinked ones for every architecture under both
linkings --- 14 of 14. \textbf{We got 3 of 14.} Grouping by terminal helped \textbf{0 of 7}; grouping by
customer helped 3. We further predicted that terminal grouping would beat customer grouping for at least
5 of 7 architectures, because we had \emph{measured} terminal windows to be far more fraud-dense.
\textbf{It wins for 1 of 7, and the sign is backwards.}

\textbf{The measurement that motivated the prediction is not in doubt --- the grid reproduces it.} A
transaction whose predecessor is fraudulent is 1.01 times as likely to be fraud under unlinked windows,
\textbf{13.4} times under customer windows and \textbf{72.1} times under terminal windows. So the
strongest concentration of fraud we can construct is also the one that trains the worst models: terminal
windows lose MCC for all seven architectures, and even plain unlinked windows beat them for all seven.
\textbf{Fraud adjacency does not predict learnable signal here; it inverts.} This directly contradicts
what BankSim told us, where linked windows helped every architecture. \textbf{We do not know why, and we
are not going to guess} --- but it is the third result in this one suite pointing the same way: the
customer split did not strengthen the models either, and neither defence can separate a poisoned bank.

\textbf{On our own architecture, the news is mixed and does not rescue it.} We predicted ADTCN would not
rank first under any ordering, and it does not --- 2nd, 4th and 6th of 7. That is the one Handbook
prediction of the four that held. But we also predicted ADTCN would not beat its own no-attention
ablation on either linked ordering, and on terminal windows it does, by $+0.0068$. ADTCN's 2nd place
under unlinked windows is its best placing anywhere in this project; it is also on the ordering we treat
as the control, it falls to 4th and 6th once linkage is applied, and DTCN passes it on terminal.
\textbf{The architecture verdict is unchanged.}
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}{The ULB ablation was regenerated --- and its numbers moved}
The quotable list (Section~\ref{sec:quotable}) told us not to use the ULB federated ablation until the
repaired search had been re-run. It was re-run on 12 September. The search now picks 117 hidden units and
203 steps per epoch, where the 30 August run used 142 and 76, and the ULB column moved:
\begin{itemize}
  \item FedAvg went from 0.569 to 0.425 MCC;
  \item FedAvg+Krum went from 0.776 to 0.751, so Krum's advantage with no attacker grew from $+0.207$ to

  \item \textbf{the privacy collapse flipped direction.} The old collapsed model flagged nothing; the new
  one flags 43{,}089 transactions, and accuracy falls 75\,pp.
\end{itemize}
So the \emph{direction} of a privacy collapse is not a property of the dataset: it changed between two ULB

figures in Section~\ref{sec:comparisons} use the regenerated file; the old one is kept in version control
(commit \texttt{ccdd19f}).
\end{warnbox}

\begin{decision}{18 --- AMLSim is too small for one sweep's fixed design}
\qlabel{Question raised:} The scalability sweep gives each of up to 20 banks 8{,}000 training rows.
AMLSim's training period holds 138{,}514, room for 17, so the job crashed after 5.5 seconds. Shrink every
bank's share until 20 fit, drop the sweep, or stop the range where the data runs out?

\qlabel{Decision taken:} Stop the fast estimator's range at 16 banks instead of 20, keep everything else,
and write the change into \texttt{TASK.md} before any AMLSim scalability number existed.

\qlabel{Why:} shrinking the shares would change the controlled variable at every federation size and break
comparability with the other datasets. Dropping the sweep would throw away both pre-registered statements,
which are read on 3 to 12 banks. Because banks are cut in order with fixed seeds, every size that is kept
is identical to what the full design computes --- checked bit for bit on AMLSim and on ULB.

\qlabel{Outcome:} the re-run finished cleanly, and both statements came out as pre-registered on PaySim
and AMLSim. The exact calculation's cost is structural: 69.6 and 62.0\,s for 12 banks on Kaggle, against
113.5 to 140.3\,s on the laptop --- a difference of machine, not of data. The fast estimator still picks the
top contributor only intermittently, at 3 of 10 sizes on both.
\end{decision}

\begin{decision}{19 --- A pre-registered criterion scores differently under two readings}
\qlabel{Question raised:} AMLSim's item (c) says the output channel inverts ``less often'' than the
weight channel. Read strictly, that holds at 8 of 11 noise levels, below the threshold of 10. Read as ``no
more often'' --- the statistic that scored every earlier condition --- it holds at 11 of 11. The three
disputed levels are ties where both channels rank perfectly. Call it held?

\qlabel{Decision taken (team lead):} No verdict. Wherever two readings of a pre-registered criterion
disagree, the report gives both scores and no held/not-held label.

\qlabel{Why:} the strict wording was a drafting error --- under it, every earlier condition would also
score only 9 of 11 --- but a pre-registration is not reworded after its result is known.
\end{decision}

\section{Closing assessment}

Six days ago the work had one genuine contribution standing on one dataset, an unexamined search sitting
at its centre, and a number quoted as measured that had never been measured.

the fact is identical, but who found it first is not.

\begin{keybox}{The honest summary, for the supervisor}
\textbf{The work is smaller than it looked on 30 August, and considerably harder to argue with.}

now holds on two datasets across three different federation setups, and it rests on two statements that
do not depend on any arbitrary cut-off, rather than on a ratio that moved every time we redefined it.

Everything that did not survive is written down as not having survived, in Section~\ref{sec:quotable},
in the language a reader would need to check it.

\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{What it means, in plain language} \emph{\footnotesize (continued)} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{\color{steel}The system and its parts}} \\
\textbf{Federated learning} & Training one shared model across several organisations \emph{without} any of them handing over their data. Each trains locally; only the trained numbers are shared. \\
\textbf{FL-ADTCN} & The whole system in this thesis: federated ADTCN detectors + differential privacy + Krum + Shapley + DB-BOA + reinforcement-learning leader election, on a Hyperledger Fabric blockchain. Six parts, six promises. \\
\textbf{ADTCN} & Our fraud detector. It reads a window of 10 consecutive transactions and looks for patterns across them. Measured honestly as a component: 5th of 7 on ULB, 7th of 7 on BankSim's customer-linked windows. It is not a title claim and does not have to win. \\
\textbf{FedAvg} \newline (Federated Averaging) & \textbf{The standard way to combine what the banks send back, and the baseline everything is compared against.} Take all the banks' weights and average them number by number; send the average back to everyone; repeat. Simple and effective --- but an average has no defence against a participant who lies, because averaging includes everybody by construction. That weakness is why Krum exists. See the box in Section~3. \\
\textbf{Krum} & The alternative to averaging. Instead of blending all the banks together, it picks the \emph{single most typical} submission and uses only that, so a sabotaged one is never included. This is a \emph{security} mechanism, not a fairness one. \\
\textbf{Shapley value} & A way of splitting credit fairly among collaborators, borrowed from game theory: how much worse would the result be if this bank had not taken part? Used here to decide token rewards. This is a \emph{fairness} mechanism, not a security one. \\
\textbf{DB-BOA} & Dynamic Butterfly--Billiards Optimisation Algorithm. The automated search, taken from the base paper, that picks the detector's settings. The framework is named after it; Section~\ref{sec:obj13} is the audit of it. \\
\textbf{Surrogate} \newline (the ``stand-in'' model) & The small, fast model DB-BOA trains in order to \emph{score} a candidate setting. It is not the model that gets deployed, and most of Section~\ref{sec:obj13} turns on keeping the two apart. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{\color{steel}Privacy and rewards}} \\
\textbf{Differential privacy (DP)}, \textbf{\eps} & Deliberate random noise added before sharing, so nothing shared can be traced back to an individual customer. \eps{} is the dial: \textbf{smaller \eps{} = more noise = more privacy but a worse model.} \\
\textbf{Weight channel vs output channel} & \emph{Where} that noise gets added --- to the model weights a bank shares, or to the contribution score it publishes. Choosing the second over the first is the thesis's actual contribution. \\
\textbf{Rank inversion} & The reward ranking coming out in the wrong order once noise is added --- i.e.\ a bank gets paid as though it contributed more than it really did. \\
\textbf{\epsst} & The heaviest noise setting at which we still \emph{caught} a rank inversion. Section~\ref{sec:obj17} explains why this number is not stable and why we stopped quoting it. \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textit{\color{steel}Data and experiment design}} \\
\textbf{ULB} & Our first dataset. 284{,}807 real anonymised card transactions, 0.17\% fraud, \textbf{no customer column}. Section~\ref{sec:fivedatasets}. \\
\textbf{BankSim} & Our second dataset. 594{,}643 simulated transactions, 1.211\% fraud, and crucially \textbf{4{,}112 identifiable customers}, so a window can be one person's own history. \\
\textbf{Fraud Detection Handbook} & Our third dataset: 1.75\,M simulated card transactions timed to the second, with customer \emph{and} terminal IDs. \\
\textbf{PaySim} & A simulated mobile-money dataset (6.36\,M transactions). No banks, and senders almost never repeat; we use the 2.77\,M rows of the two types that contain fraud. \\
\textbf{IBM AMLSim} & A simulator of money laundering between accounts at several banks. We generated 198{,}015 transactions with three native banks. Its label is \emph{laundering}, not fraud. \\
\textbf{Stratified vs entity-disjoint split} & Two ways of dealing transactions out to the three banks. \emph{Stratified:} shuffle everything and deal equally, keeping the fraud ratio the same --- so the same customer can land in two banks. \emph{Entity-disjoint:} deal out whole \emph{customers}, so nobody's history is split across banks. The second is far more realistic. \\
\textbf{Window (global vs entity-linked)} & The 10 transactions the detector reads at once. \emph{Global:} whatever 10 rows came next in the file, probably 10 different people. \emph{Entity-linked:} 10 transactions by the \emph{same} customer. The difference between these two is worth more than any architecture choice we tested. \\
\textbf{Confound control} & An extra run that deliberately changes only \emph{one} factor, so an effect can be pinned on a cause. The one in Section~\ref{sec:control} cost three hours and killed our best claim. \\
\textbf{Pre-registration} & Writing down what you expect \emph{and} what being wrong would mean, before running --- then recording the result underneath without editing the prediction. \\
\textbf{Common random numbers} & Scoring every candidate on the \emph{same} random sample, so the comparison between them is not corrupted by luck of the draw. The most transferable finding of the period (Section~\ref{sec:obj13}). \\
\bottomrule
\end{xltabular}}

\begin{keybox}{Practical notes that cost us time to learn --- read before running anything}
\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Use the PowerShell runners, not the shell scripts.} On this machine \texttt{python} is not
on Git Bash's path at all, and the one that \emph{is} on the path does not have \texttt{torch}
installed. You will get a confusing error rather than an obvious one.
\item \textbf{Set \texttt{PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8} when redirecting output, \emph{and} open files with an
explicit encoding.} These are two separate traps that produce similar-looking failures, and fixing the
first does not fix the second.
\item \textbf{Launch anything multi-hour detached, with a pre-flight check first.} The pre-flight
asserts the code is in the state you think it is before spending the CPU. It costs seconds, and it is
the only reason the 16-hour run finished with no surprises in it.
\item \textbf{Every detector number is meaningless without a pinned torch version and thread count.}
This is not a formality --- Section~7.1 is the story of what happens when they differ.
\item \textbf{Never put the Kaggle token on a command line.} It lives only in the git-ignored
\texttt{kaggle.md}; the tools read it into the child process's environment.
\item \textbf{Kaggle drops empty folders and ignored our thread setting.} The job script now recreates its
output folders and sets torch's thread count explicitly. Both were found by a test job, not by reading.
\item \textbf{Upstream Python on Windows writes doubled line endings} (\texttt{\textbackslash r\textbackslash r\textbackslash n}),
which AMLSim's Java half reads as empty records. The generation pipeline repairs them and proves that
nothing else changed.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}
